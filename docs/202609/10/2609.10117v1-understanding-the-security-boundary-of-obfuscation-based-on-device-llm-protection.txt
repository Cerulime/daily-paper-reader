## Abstract 

Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) offer a promising mecha-nism for safeguarding the intellectual property of on-device Large Language Models (LLMs). To overcome the inherent computational bottlenecks of TEEs, existing TEE-Shielded LLM Partition (TSLP) methods apply efficient obfuscation schemes to computationally intensive layers, offloading them to external GPUs while retaining only lightweight operations within the TEE. Although a grow-ing body of TSLP-based approaches has emerged, these defense mechanisms remain largely heuristic. Consequently, some meth-ods are proven vulnerable to certain specialized adversarial attacks designed to exploit their specific architectural implementations. To overcome the limitations of these heuristic designs, this pa-per addresses a fundamental research question: can we establish common primitives to unify representative prior methodologies, characterize the security boundary of their compositions , and systematically extend them? To this end, we formalize a set of obfuscation primitives, defined as dual-tuples of linear computa-tions satisfying specific algebraic properties. We demonstrate that the matrix-level weight transformations of the representative ef-ficient TSLP frameworks studied in this paper can be expressed as compositions of these primitives; consequently, the canonical form of these primitive compositions , denoted as Oprior , charac-terizes the structural boundary of this primitive family. We then expose the vulnerabilities of Oprior through a novel primitive-guided attack methodology, Collapse, demonstrating a shared vulnera-bility in several prominent TSLP methods published in top-tier venues, such as ArrowCloak (Security’25), TSQP (S&P’25), and LoRO (NeurIPS’25) . Finally, we introduce two novel obfuscation primitives and integrate them with existing constructs to formulate 

Oext , extending this security boundary .

## 1 Introduction 

Large language models (LLMs) [2, 32, 35] are increasingly deployed on end devices to reduce inference latency while keeping user data on-device [8, 9, 17, 23]. Because these models are valuable proprietary assets, placing the model weights on end devices raises significant concerns regarding model theft. Compared to cloud-based LLM serving, a local adversary could obtain white-box visibility into code, parameters, memory, and intermediate activations of the on-device LLMs that the cloud-based APIs would never expose, resulting in model-extraction [3, 12, 13, 19, 20, 34] and privacy attacks [7, 27, 38]. Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) such as Intel SGX [4] and Arm TrustZone [22] provide hardware-isolated execution even when the operating system is untrusted, making them suitable for securing the on-device models. However, commodity TEEs are not scalable to host full LLM inference on their own: secure memory is limited, and their compute throughput falls far short of what modern LLM serving requires. Recent works therefore adopt a TEE-Shielded LLM Partition (TSLP) methods [15, 16, 18, 26, 33, 39, 43, 49], in which the TEE computes the lightweight non-linear computations, (e.g., LayerNorm, Softmax), while the compute-intensive linear operators (i.e., matrix multiplications) are offloaded to the surround-ing Rich Execution Environments (REEs), such as the commodity GPU/NPU runtime. As these REEs are not trusted to be secure, the TEEs are required to obfuscate the model weights (for instance via various matrix manipulation tricks) before offloading them to REEs. The REEs then compute on the obfuscated inputs, and the TEE subsequently recovers the correct results by “subtracting” the ob-fuscation factors from the results. Existing research on TSLP has developed along two parallel lines: developing new obfuscation schemes and exploiting the vulnerabili-ties of these schemes. However, existing obfuscation methodologies are largely ad-hoc. Each obfuscation scheme invents tricks based on certain efficiently-computable algebraic operations, such as column-wise permutation [16], column-wise scaling [26, 30], additive masking [31, 33, 49], or low-rank masking [43], and applies them to masking the offloaded linear layers. For instance, one recent design ArrowCloak [39] hand-stack several opera-tions. In each case, the authors show that a handful of obvious inversions fail against the chosen tricks and declare the scheme secure. Meanwhile, attacks against these mechanisms are likewise ad-hoc, where each targets a specific class of obfuscation methods. Zhang et al. [47] target schemes built on additive masking [33, 49], and ArrowMatch [39] targets schemes built on column-wise permutation and scaling [16, 26].

To overcome the limitations of these heuristic designs, this pa-per addresses a fundamental research question: can we establish common primitives to unify representative prior methodologies, characterize the security boundary of their compositions , and sys-tematically extend them? To this end, we formalize a set of 

obfuscation primitives , defined as dual-tuples of linear computa-tions satisfying specific algebraic properties. We demonstrate that the matrix-level weight transformations studied in several promi-nent TSLP methods published in top-tier venues [16, 26, 30, 31, 39, 43, 48, 49] can be expressed as compositions of these primitives. Concretely, we identify two categories of primitives, multiplicative primitives (permutation Π and scaling 𝐷 ) and additive primitives (sparse mask 𝑆 and low-rank mask 𝐿 ). Each primitive is invertible and efficiently-computable, so any combination of them is also efficiently-computable within TEE’s secure enclave. Given these unified primitives, we identify Oprior as a canonical structural form for the family generated by these primitives, which represents the security boundary of these methods . Yet, we demonstrate that Oprior is vulnerable, revealing a shared vulnerability in these approaches. Specifically, we develop a novel attack methodology Collapse against Oprior . Our key insight is that the column directions of the protected model weight 𝑊 vic cannot be effectively masked by ar-bitrary composition of these primitives. Specifically, column-wise permutation Π and column-wise scaling 𝐷 only permute and rescale columns, so the column directions of Mvic are preserved and re-main correlated with the public Mpre . Second, sparse mask 𝑆 only masks a tiny fraction of elements in Mvic , so the column direction of Mvic are largely intact. Third, low-rank mask 𝐿 hides Mvic in-side one low-dimensional subspace V𝐿 , so the column directions of Mvic are intact in the subspace orthogonal to V𝐿 . As a result, we can apply a systematic methodology to leverage such leaked information to obtain a surrogate model Msur with the same archi-tecture and comparable performance with Mvic . At a high level, our attack methodology has three steps: aligning the permutations of multiple observations of Mvic ; combining the aligned observations to recover the low-rank mask 𝐿 and locating the positions that has been masked by the sparse mask 𝑆 to obtain an intermediate model 

Minit ; fine-tuning Minit on a small labeled dataset (collected by querying Mvic ) to remove the remaining sparse mask 𝑆 and scaling 

𝐷 , resulting in Msur .To further extend this security boundary , we propose two novel obfuscation primitives: the sparse multiplicative primitive mixes the columns of Mvic and double-sided multiplicative primitive disturbs both column and row of Mvic . Combining these two primitives with existing primitives yields Oext , which disrupts the row- and column-wise invariants on which Collapse relies . Extensive ex-periments across four representative models (e.g., BERT-Base, ViT-Base, Qwen2.5-0.5B, and Qwen2.5-1.5B) demonstrate that while Collapse can extract high-fidelity surrogate models from prior defenses, Oext reduces the resulting surrogate accuracy to the em-pirical black-box reference on average. We clarify that we do not claim the universal security of Oext . In-stead, we only evaluate Oext against the existing Collapse pipeline. Since we only evaluate Oext against existing pipeline without a defense-specific adaptation, we do not claim the universal secu-rity of Oext . Yet, our main contribution is to demonstrate a unified methodology to understand the security of obfuscation-based on-device LLM protection. Concretely, the contributions of our work are as follows. 

• Unified Obfuscation Primitives in TSLP Approaches. 

We formalize a set of obfuscation primitives that underpin the matrix-level weight transformations of several repre-sentative TSLP methods published in top-tier venues , pro-viding a common algebraic abstraction for understanding and analyzing these approaches within a single framework. 

• Identify the Security Boundary. Building on the uni-fied analysis abstraction, we define Oprior as a canonical structural form for the family generated by the existing primitives, which represents the security boundary of these methods . 

• Breaking the Security Boundary. We develop Collapse, a systematic, primitive-guided attack against Oprior and its instantiations, demonstrating a common vulnerability shared by those TSLP approaches. 

• Extending the Boundary. Guided by Collapse, we pro-pose Oext to further extend this security boundary beyond 

Oprior by introducing novel obfuscation primitives. We eval-uate both Collapse and Oext across representative models, datasets, and obfuscation schemes. 

## 2 Background and Threat Model 2.1 Transformer Inference and TEE–REE Collaborative Inference 

2.1.1 Operator Classes in Transformer Inference. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a transformer block contains three operator classes that differ on what matters for protection—whether they carry proprietary weights and whether they dominate arithmetic cost. Op linear com-prises the weight-bearing linear transforms (QKV, output projec-tion, feed-forward-network (FFN) up/down), which carry the propri-etary parameters. Op quadra comprises the token-to-token products inside self-attention, whose cost grows quadratically with sequence length. Op non-poly collects the remaining lightweight non-linear operators (normalization, activation, residual). 

2.1.2 TEE–REE Collaborative Inference. Trusted Execution Envi-ronments such as Intel SGX [4] and Arm TrustZone [22] enforce code and data isolation even when the OS is untrusted, but have limited secure memory and weaker throughput than the accel-erators used for modern inference. Recent systems therefore re-tain the lightweight Op non-poly stages inside the TEE and offload Op linear and Op quadra to the untrusted Rich Execution Environment (REE) [15, 16, 18, 26, 33, 39, 47]. A weight-bearing linear layer would expose its parameters in clear, so every scheme we study interposes an obfuscation step. For input X and protected weight W, the TEE releases eW = O( W), the REE returns X eW, and the TEE applies a recovery map R satisfying R ( X eW) = XW .

## 3 Systematizing Linear Obfuscation 3.1 Obfuscation Primitives 

The fundamental building blocks of this pipeline are what we term 

Obfuscation Primitives , which abstract the pair of operations used to mask a weight matrix in the REE and to recover the correct result inside the TEE. A trivial instantiation is an i.i.d. Gaussian mask 

𝑅 , with O( 𝑊 ) = 𝑊 + 𝑅 and R ( 𝑋 e𝑊 ) = 𝑋 e𝑊 − 𝑋 𝑅 . ## 3.2 From Primitives to Schemes 

A central property of obfuscation primitives is that primitives com-pose without degrading any of the three properties: correctness remains exact, TEE-side computation cost grows only additively, and under the conditions stated below, the composition is no easier to extract than individual components . 

Theorem 1 (Primitives are closed under composition) . Let P =

(O 1, R1) and Q = (O 2, R2) be compatible obfuscation primitives . Their composition Q ◦ P is the pair (O 2 ◦ O 1, R1 ◦ R 2), where obfuscations are applied in order and recoveries in reverse so that each R𝑖 undoes its corresponding O𝑖 . Under the conditions below, this composed pair is itself a valid obfuscation primitive, with: 

(i) Exact correctness , by chaining the correctness of P and Q;

(ii) Additive TEE-side cost , 𝐶 (Q ◦ P) = 𝐶 (P) + 𝐶 (Q) , still negligible relative to 𝐶 (𝑋𝑊 );

(iii) No weaker model-extraction resistance . Intuitively, an attack that extracts the composition would also induce an attack on each component. Under the formal full-transcript simulation conditions in Appendix F, the composition is there-fore no easier to extract than either P or Q .

## 3.4 Security Boundary of Bprior 

§ 3.3 established that the matrix-level transformations listed in Table 2 lie in F (B prior ). This motivates the following question: does this family admit a “most secure” composition ? 

Definition 2 (Security Boundary) . Let B be a base set of obfus-cation primitives. A scheme O∗ is the security boundary of B if it satisfies the efficiency requirement in Definition 1 and every efficiency-preserving P ∈ F (B) can be realized by O∗ under some choice of parameters. Therefore, O∗ can be viewed as the “most secure” scheme attainable from B.

## 4 Attack Intuition 

Before presenting Collapse in detail (§5), we first explain why Oprior 

is vulnerable. The high-level message is simple: no matter how the four primitives {Π, 𝐷, 𝑆, 𝐿 } are composed, the column directions of the victim weight 𝑊 vic remain largely exposed. We articulate this as three insights, each pointing to a structural limitation of one or more primitives. 

Insight 1 (Column atomicity). 

Each victim column survives in e𝑊 as a transformed copy of a single original column. Π and 𝐷 relabel and rescale columns but never mix them. 

Π permutes columns and 𝐷 scales them entry-wise per column; nei-ther operation forms linear combinations across columns. Hence the set of column directions of 𝑊 vic passes through Π and 𝐷 unchanged, up to reordering and positive rescaling. 

Insight 2 (Sparse coverage gap). 

A vanishing fraction of entries are perturbed; the rest leak in cleartext. Sparse mask 𝑆 has nnz ≪ 𝑛 2 nonzeros. Practical deployments keep 𝑆 extremely sparse for efficiency, so nearly every entry of each column of 𝑊 vic is released verbatim. A column direction is a global property of all 𝑛 entries, and corrupting only a small fraction of them barely tilts it. 

Insight 3 (Low-rank coverage gap). 

Each column’s (𝑛 −𝑟 )-dim orthogonal component leaks in cleartext. Low-rank mask 𝐿 = 𝐴𝐵 ⊤ confines noise to an 

𝑟 -dim subspace. Because 𝐿 has rank at most 𝑟 ≪ 𝑛 , every column of 𝐿 lives in a single 𝑟 -dimensional subspace. Projecting away from this subspace removes 𝐿 entirely and exposes the remaining (𝑛 −𝑟 ) dimensions of each victim column without any noise at all. 

Why direction leakage suffices. Oprior fails to hide the column di-rections of 𝑊 vic . Since the backbone architecture is public, building 

Msur reduces to recovering the weight matrices. Once the column directions are obtained, the attacker only needs to fix them and fine-tune the column magnitudes to build Msur . §5 develops the attack.

## 6 Extending the Security Boundary 

The leakage exposed by Collapse traces to a single invariant of 

Oprior : the column directions of 𝑊 survive the obfuscation . None of the primitives in Bprior removes this invariant; to push the bound-ary forward, the base set itself must be enlarged with primitives that obfuscate column (and, symmetrically, row) directions directly. § 6.1 introduces the sparse multiplicative primitive P𝑀 that mixes columns of 𝑊 , and § 6.2 introduces the double-sided multiplicative primitive P𝐷𝑆 that simultaneously mixes rows. § 6.3 writes down the canonical form Oext of the extended family, and proves it is the new security boundary.

GPU-based confidential computing is a promising direction for protecting accelerator-side execution, with proposals such as Graviton [36] and ACAI [29] extending hardware-isolated execution to the accelerator (see [40] for a survey of the CPU– GPU confidential-computing landscape). However, it depends on specific hardware, firmware, and deployment support, and is not yet available on many commodity devices. Our work studies the current TEE–REE setting where the CPU-side TEE is trusted while the GPU/NPU remains untrusted. Thus, our primitive-based analysis is complementary to GPU TEEs: confidential GPUs can reduce the exposed boundary when available, while our results characterize the security of algorithmic obfuscation when accelerators are not trusted. 

12 Understanding the Security Boundary of Obfuscation-based On-Device LLM Protection 

Side-channel attacks. We assume the TEE correctly protects enclave-resident code and data, and do not consider hardware side channels or transient-execution attacks against the TEE. A separate body of work recovers model weights through fault injection or memory/PCIe side channels—e.g., RowHammer-based weight extraction in DeepSteal [25], GPU memory-bus snooping in HyperTheft [44] and CipherSteal [45], PCIe-traffic analysis in the Hermes attack [50], and the side-channel reverse-engineering of CNNs by Hua et al. [11]. This line is orthogonal to our focus: our attacks do not rely on compromising the TEE or any hardware channel; they exploit algebraic structure in the obfuscated tensors already exposed to the REE. Existing TEE and bus-side side-channel mitigations can therefore be combined with our framework. 

Key rotation and boundary-visible leakage. Our analysis reveals a counterintuitive consequence of lightweight obfuscation. Because these primitives conceal only selected algebraic aspects of the weight matrix, a single obfuscated view can still reveal exploitable structure. Refreshing the keys produces multiple differently obfus-cated views; comparing them may reveal complementary clues and make weight recovery easier. Thus, under our threat model, more frequent key rotation can weaken resistance to model extraction. Appendix G analyzes the fully refreshed setting. 

Defense scope and adaptive attacks. Our evidence for Oext is em-pirical and limited to the existing Collapse pipeline without a defense-specific adaptation. Developing defense-aware attacks tar-geting 𝑀 𝑋 and 𝑀 𝑊 is left for future work; we claim no formal model-extraction security guarantee. 

Other TSLP Schemes. GroupCover [48] represents a different security–efficiency trade-off. By mixing groups of weight vectors through matrix multiplication, it can hide stronger column-level invariants than efficient TSLP schemes. However, it splits into mix-ing groups based on distances between weight vectors, rather than randomly choosing groups. Since fine-tuned weights are often close to the public pretrained weights, 𝑊 pre may help the attacker guess which vectors are mixed together. ShadowNet [31] uses a dense ad-ditive mask, but the mask is not fully confined within the TEE: part of the mask-related information is exposed to the GPU to enable efficient outsourced computation. This exposure gives the attacker extra information to steal the protected model. 

## 9 Related Work 

TEE-shielded model partition. TEE-shielded partition schemes split a model between TEE and REE; early designs such as Dark-neTZ [18] and Occlumency [15] simply partition without obfus-cating offloaded weights. To better protect the offloaded weights, subsequent schemes combine algebraic tricks heuristically: Shad-owNet [31] composes permutation with random per-channel scal-ing on convolution filters; Magnitude [10] partitions selected high-magnitude weights into the TEE ; NNSplitter [49] perturbs a small fraction ( ≈ 0.002%) of weight entries and stores their original values in the TEE; TransLinkGuard [16] couples layer-specific row/column permutations with TEE authorization that carries permuted acti-vations across adjacent transformer layers ; Soter [26] morphs op-erators with scalar blinding coins and restores accumulated coins at enclave boundaries; TSQP [30] combines a learned dissimilar model with quantization scaling; LoRO [43] obfuscates weights with a dense mask generated from low-rank factors; ArrowCloak [39] composes permutation, scaling, and a low-rank mask. A common primitive-level structural characterization of these schemes is lack-ing . 

Attacks on obfuscation-based TSLP. Existing attacks rely heav-ily on expert intuition and lack a unified methodology. Zhang et al. [47] taxonomize TSDP schemes by which layers are shielded, without analyzing the algebraic structure of the obfuscation ap-plied to offloaded layers. ArrowMatch [39] attacks schemes built on permutation and scaling by matching obfuscated columns against a public prior, with an attack design specific to these two primitives. LoRO [43] similarly attacks permutation-, scaling-, and sparse-mask-based schemes by exploiting their statistical proximity to a public prior, but its attack design relies heavily on hand-crafted heuristics. 

Our work. We move the design of TSLP attacks and defenses beyond expert intuition. To this end, we introduce a unified alge-braic abstraction over the representative obfuscation primitives studied in this paper . From this abstraction we derive a canonical structural form for the family generated by these primitives and a systematic attack against it, and extend the primitive set with two new constructs that close the structural channels exploited by the attack. 

## 10 Conclusion 

The rapid evolution of heuristic attacks and defenses for TEE-shielded LLM partitioning (TSLP) has substantially advanced this area, but it has also led to a fragmented design space dominated by point solutions. As a result, existing schemes are difficult to compare, evaluate, and extend in a principled manner. In this paper, we take a step toward systematizing this design space by introduc-ing Unified Obfuscation Primitives, a primitive-level abstraction that captures representative defense mechanisms for TSLP. This abstraction enables us to identify a canonical structural boundary for the declared primitive family . Building on this foundation, we systematically attack this canonical form and extend the declared primitive family . Our evaluation demonstrates that the proposed attack can effectively recover the hidden obfuscation structure and build high-fidelity surrogate models across diverse obfuscation fam-ilies, revealing fundamental limitations of lightweight obfuscation in TSLP systems.

## B Ethical Considerations 

This paper develops a model-extraction attack against a family of deployed defenses for on-device LLM inference. Publishing such work carries dual-use risk: the same pipeline that demonstrates the insecurity of Oprior could help a malicious device owner steal proprietary weights in practice. We conclude that disclosure is justified and that the risks are outweighed by the benefits, for four reasons. (i) The defenses we break maybe already deployed; Table 2 lists peer-reviewed schemes from 2022–2025 at top-tier venues. Systematically characterizing their insecurity across the six canonicalized schemes is more ac-tionable than yet another scheme-specific attack. (ii) Our threat model assumes a physically present, trusted-hardware-excluded adversary; the attack does not extend to cloud-only or API-only deployments. (iii) We provide a concrete defense ( Oext , § 6) that, in our experiments, reduces the attacker’s advantage over ordinary fine-tuning by an order of magnitude. (iv) We do not publish pre-extracted weights of any proprietary model; the evaluation uses publicly available pretrained checkpoints throughout, and our ar-tifacts contain only the defense and attack implementations, not extracted victim parameters. No live production system was targeted, and no personally iden-tifiable or sensitive data was used in any experiment.

Stage 1: permutation alignment and support localization. The original LDD test computes the rank of two column blocks using all rows.
