Abstract —Effective source recovery is critical to security ap-plications such as malware analysis, vulnerability assessment, and legacy maintenance. Large Language Models (LLMs) are reshaping this field, shifting the paradigm away from rule-based heuristics to probabilistic and high fidelity semantic recovery of source code from assembly or classical decompiler-derived pseudo-C. However, despite rapid progress, the field suffers from fragmentation across numerous approaches as well as their non-unified evaluations, limiting objective comparisons. Further, existing works have limited coverage of embedded, IoT architectures and source languages beyond C/C++. In this work, we present the first Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) focused specifically on LLM-assisted binary-to-source recovery. We provide a granular design-centric taxonomy of LLM-assisted source recovery methods and systematic evaluations using seven key metrics along six evaluation dimensions. We evaluate state-of-the-art methods on 45,000 test samples derived from four standard and five embedded architectures, five optimization levels, and symbol stripping. We ablate the impact of design choices on recovery performance, including input representa-tion, contextual enrichment, model scale, iteration and review roles using controlled in-house recovery pipelines and three off-the-shelf models. Finally, we test same-language and cross-language recovery capability covering four mature and two legacy languages. Our systematization and comprehensive evaluations provide key insights that guide future directions in this field. 

Index Terms —Source Code Recovery, Large Language Models, Reliability, Reverse Engineering, Binary Decompilation 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Source code recovery is a critical building block of reverse engineering, computer security, and software maintenance. It enables security analysts to inspect and investigate pro-grams [68], analyze malware [81], audit third-party binaries for vulnerabilities [57], plagiarism detection [73], and so on, all without access to original source code. Traditional decom-pilers such as Hex-Rays [3], Binary Ninja [1], Ghidra [2], and Angr [58] typically operate on a pipeline of deterministic Intermediate Representations (IR), control flow reconstruction algorithms, pattern matching and type inference heuristics. While these classical tools preserve lower-level control exe-cution semantics, their output is marred by issues like poor readability, unnatural control constructs, lost variable names, and fragmented type structures, which ultimately hinders hu-man analysis and recompilation [25]. The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) trained on massive language and code corpora has led to unprece-dented capabilities in code generation, analysis, and software comprehension. Building upon this proficiency, LLMs are now increasingly being used to address the challenges in reverse engineering [40], [56], [72]. Rather than relying purely on deterministic rules, LLM-assisted source recovery models treat decompilation or pseudo-C refinement as a neural sequence-to-sequence translation task that converts low-level repre-sentations to recompilable and equivalent high-level source code. These approaches show promise in recovering idiomatic function names, expressive variable and type structures, and clean high-level abstractions directly from assembly or de-compiled pseudo-C output [41], [55], [64], [65], [69], [71], [72]. Despite rapid proliferation across top security, software engineering, and AI venues, the research landscape remains highly fragmented. Even when restricting the scope to top-tier venues (see Section III-B) over the past two years, 13 distinct methodologies have been published [6], [16], [26], [31], [38], [41], [46], [61]–[64], [70], [78], alongside several pre-prints this year [21], [32], [44], [49], [59], [77]. Prior systematizations broadly explore LLM applications in reverse engineering with coarse taxonomies of LLM-based source recovery [7], [39], [48]. To the best of our knowledge, there is no existing systematization of the granular design choices of LLM-assisted source recovery methods, and no controlled ablations of such choices to measure their impact on fidelity. Further, evaluation metrics are highly fragmented, with 53 unique metrics identified across 20+ research papers in top venues on LLM-assisted source recovery from 2024–2026. Moreover, existing evaluations and benchmarks on LLM-assisted source recovery focus almost exclusively on x86-64 and C/C++ target generation [33], [65], [76], with coverage of non-C languages only by a few method papers and limited to C++ [46], [70], CUDA [62], and Dart [5]. Further, decom-pilation fidelity across legacy and mature languages has only been studied for pre-LLM neural decompiations [37]. There is no existing evaluation of LLM-assisted source recovery that covers embedded systems architectures (armv7m, armv8m, armv7a, armv8a, and mips23le) [66]. To bridge this gap, this paper presents the first comprehen-sive Systematization of Knowledge (SoK) dedicated to LLM-assisted source recovery. II. P RELIMINARIES 

To contextualize the shift from deterministic heuristics to neural source code recovery, this section outlines the funda-mental mechanics of information loss during compilation and the differences between classical and neural decompilers. 

A. The Lossy Nature of Compilation 

Compilation is an inherently destructive, one-way trans-formation where a decompiler cannot recover the original source deterministically [24]. When high-level source code is translated into a machine-executable binary, the compiler strips away rich semantic abstractions such as meaningful variable names, custom data structures, class hierarchies, and idiomatic control flow constructs [20], [43], [45], [54]. Furthermore, aggressive compiler optimizations can significantly refactor the underlying program logic to maximize hardware efficiency, through, for example, loop unrolling, function inlining, and dead code elimination [8], [9], [23], [75]. Consequently, recovering the original source code is a challenging task, rooted in the many-to-one nature of the compilation pro-cess [14], [27], [50]. The practical goal of decompilation is therefore to recover a functionally equivalent and human-readable representation for a variety of use cases that include automated and manual analysis for vulnerability detection, patch diffing, malware analysis, program comprehension, and software license compliance [17], [18], [64].

They typically disassemble raw machine code, lift the as-sembly instructions into an Intermediate Representation (IR), and perform extensive data-flow and control-flow graph (CFG) analyses [4], [15], [74]. Finally, heuristic-based structural anal-ysis and type inference are applied to emit C-like pseudo-C. While mathematically sound and highly effective at preserving low-level execution semantics, these tools are brittle. Failure could occur when encountering heavily optimized code, non-standard calling conventions, or obfuscated binaries, often producing pseudo-C cluttered with unnatural goto statements and fragmented types that inhibit human comprehension [13], [18], [75]. 

C. The Neural Decompiler Paradigm 

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) frames source code recovery not as a problem of deterministic graph reduction, but as a neural translation task. By leveraging transformer architectures pre-trained on vast corpora of lan-guage, code, and assembly, neural decompilers attempt to probabilistically infer the lost semantic context. Instead of relying on hardcoded heuristics, these models ingest assembly language [31], [41], [64], or pseudo-C [64] emitted by classical decompilers, and output high-level source code. Because they evaluate the global context of the input sequence, LLMs excel at generating idiomatic function names, reconstructing complex nested logic, and emitting highly readable code [56]. However, this probabilistic nature introduces a critical vulnerability: hallucinations. Neural models may generate syntactically flawless code that subtly alters the functional behavior of the original binary, creating a tension between readability and semantic or functional determinism [33]. B. Systematization of LLM-assisted Source Recovery Ap-proaches in the Literature 

We characterize each research work through five-axes tax-onomy, as depicted in Fig. 1 and Table I: 1) Model setup (M): This axis depicts how the underlying LLM is prepared: off-the-shelf (M 1) [16], [21], [32], [38], [46], [70], [77], [78], fine-tuned from a checkpoint (M 2)[6], [26], [31], [49], [59], [61]–[64], or pre-trained for decompilation (M 3) [41]. 2) Control flow (C): This axis characterizes how the model is driven, including single-pass generation (C 1) [6], [26], [31], [38], [41], [46], [59], [61]–[64], [78], iterative feedback-driven repair loop (C 2) [21], [32], [49], [70], [77], or an autonomous tool-using agent (C 3) [16]. 3) Number of roles per turn (R): This axis depicts the functional decomposition of the LLM into various roles, for example, “code generator” and “reviewer”, per de-compilation pass. Types including single role (R 1) [6], [16], [26], [31], [32], [41], [49], [59], [61], [62], [64], [70], or multiple specialized roles (R 2) [21], [38], [46], [63], [77], [78]. 4) Input representation (I): This axis depicts the binary-derived forms included in the input to the LLM, covering forms such has hex, assembly, and low-level IR (I 1) [6], [16], [31], [32], [41], [49], [61], [62], [64], classical decompiler pseudo-C (I 2) [21], [26], [32], [38], [46], [49], [59], [63], [64], [70], [77], [78]), and structural enrichment such as rodata, control-flow graphs (CFG), callees, .data, and .bss (I 3) [16], [21], [26], [32], [59], [61], [78]. 5) Augmentation (A): The last axis depicts the inference-time mechanism divided into context provisioning through retrieval augmented generation (RAG, A 1) [32], [46], [78] and self-exemplar (A 2) [31]), and verifier feedback, including compiler (A 3) [16], [21], [49], [59], [70], [77], execution (A 4) [16], [21], [49], [70], [77], and semantics (A 5) [38]. 

Research in 2024-2025: The field of LLM-assisted source code recovery opened with single-pass methods, wherein a single forward generation produces the recovered source. DeGPT [38] prompts an off-the-shelf GPT-3.5 with Ghidra pseudo-C passed through a fixed three-role pipeline compris-ing a referee that decides which of structure simplification, comment insertion, or variable renaming to apply, an advisor that proposes the edits, and an operator that accepts or rejects each edit through a Micro Snippet Semantic Calculation (MSSC) gate, adding no external data beyond that semantic check. LLM4Decompile [64] fine-tunes DeepSeek-Coder into a decompiler series that translates in one pass under two input regimes, end-to-end from raw x86-64 assembly or refinement from Ghidra pseudo-C. SCCDEC [31] stays within single-pass control but adds self-augmentation: its tuning-free variant recompiles the model’s own draft into a self-constructed (assembly, source) exemplar that is prepended before a second decompilation, and a companion fine-tuned variant further aligns the model to reach its best results. Nova [41] takes the most training-heavy route, pre-training an assembly-native model from DeepSeek-Coder with hierarchical attention and contrastive objectives and decompiling stripped x86-64 assem-bly in a single pass. PtxDec [62] fine-tunes Qwen2.5-Coder-7B and Qwen3-32B to translate optimized GPU PTX, normalized into a compact rolled-PTX intermediate representation, back into readable CUDA. PseudoFix [46] prompts off-the-shelf models with decompiler pseudo-C in a fixed detect-then-refactor sweep across six structural-distortion types, retriev-ing similar examples from an external distortion database to ground each fix. The lone departure from single-pass control was DecLLM [70], which wraps off-the-shelf GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in an iterative repair loop that recompiles and re-runs each candidate to repair the input pseudo-C, feeding back compiler errors, ASan reports, and test- and fuzzing-detected output divergences for up to fifteen rounds, foreshadowing the direction the field would take a year later. 

Research in 2026: Research in 2026 brought both a diver-sification of single-pass designs and a decisive shift toward iterative control. The sole fully agentic system (i.e. featuring an LLM with tool-calling capabilities), Cao [16], deploys an off-the-shelf coding agent that autonomously reads AVR firmware disassembly and its interrupt-vector table and refines its output against avr-gcc recompilation and cycle-accurate simavr behavioral traces. Single-pass generation continued but grew richer in inputs and roles: FidelityGPT [78] runs a two-role detect-then-correct pass over pseudo-C enriched with a program-dependence graph and retrieves from an annotated distortion database. Compilation and Readability measure the general “quality” of the generated code, while Lexical , Syntactic , Semantic , and 

Functional similarity measure how ”close” or ”equivalent” (if derivable) the generated source code is to the original in terms of its appearance, structure, meaning, and behavior, respectively. The distinction between the two objectives is the comparison target: quality metrics are computed on the gen-erated code alone, or against the decompiler output a method refines, whereas similarity metrics are computed against the original program. 1) Code Quality Metrics: Among the quality metrics, prior works on LLM-assisted source recovery use 5 unique metrics for compilation : nearly every paper measures a plain Re-compilation Rate (RC) [16], [21], [31], [33], [49], [59], [61], [62], [70], [76], [77], and the rest of the variation is limited to best-of-k compilation as compile@ k [5], [6], parse-only syntax checks [77], and the repair [16], [70] and distribution [33], [61], [62] of compile errors. Since compilation is also the cheapest signal to obtain, it doubles as the feedback channel of the iterative methods discussed above. Readability is more fragmented, with 12 metrics across 9 papers. category.

Syntactic similarity exists in this literature only as CodeBLEU’s AST-match component [5], [6], [62], [65], [76]. 

Semantic similarity is the broadest and least standardized axis, with 19 metrics across 17 papers. It spans metrics for static graph comparison, including dependency equiva-lence [26], which tests dependency-graph isomorphism as an approximation of program equivalence, and CodeBLEU’s

of recompiled functions [59], and structure-fidelity correctness

V. E VALUATION METHODOLOGY 

This section describes the evaluation metrics, selected SOTA methods and models, experimental design, and the dataset used in our empirical evaluations. 

A. Evaluation Metrics Suite RQ1 

Table II highlighted the fragmentation of evaluation met-rics across research papers on LLM-assisted source recovery. None of the surveyed evaluation and method papers include an evaluation suite that captures the six dimensions along which recovered source can fail. To conduct a holistic and interpretable evaluation, we adopt one principled metric per dimension, with the exception of semantic similarity which has two metrics to cover source-derived and binary-derived semantics, resulting in a 7 metric suite described below. Re-compilation rate (RC) and Re-execution Rate (RE) are the most prevalent compilation quality and functional similar-ity metrics reported in prior works. In its traditional form, RC 

is the ratio of generated functions that compile (standalone) to the total number of evaluation functions. Our results (Fig. 4 and 15) indicate that LLMs are better at generating compilable code than recovering the correct signature. This takeaway is typically missed by RC and muddled in RE which targets functional correctness. Thus, Re-compilation Rate with caller Interface (RCI) is a valuable metric that can be computed using a benchmark’s evaluation harness. Further, RC, RCI, and RE follow a nested relationship ( RE ≤ RCI ≤ RC ): RCI and RE 

are our choice for compilation quality and functional similarity metrics, respectively. R2I [28] provides 31-readability feature composition and is increasingly being adopted as a readability metric. To standardize, we define ∆R2I as our readability quality metric to the relative R2I between a generated code ˆc and the pseudo-C of the original candidate cpc as follows: 

∆R2I (ˆ c, c pc ) = R2I (ˆ c) − R2I (cpc ). (1) A positive ∆R2I means the model’s generation is more readable than the decompiler pseudo-C an analyst would otherwise work with. We compare with the standardized decompiler output for a standardized relative quality measure instead of with noisy source code. We use Edit Similarity (ES) for lexical similarity. ES measures surface fidelity by computing the normalized Lev-enshtein distance between two code snippets reported in the range [0 , 1] , with higher values indicating closer surface form. We use CodeBLEU’s AST-match (AST) [53] to abstract-syntax-tree structure for syntactic similarity. AST is largely insensitive to the naming noise that dominates ES and provides a more specific measure of the relative syntactical structure of the recovered source. It is also the only syntactic similarity metric used in the surveyed literature in Section III. CodeBLEU’s DF-match (DF) [53] is used for semantic similarity using source-derived DF comparison: it matches def-then-use graphs, ab-stracting away both naming and syntactic idiom to test whether values flow through the recovered code as they do in the original. These three metrics compare against the original source. Byte-wise match (BW) [59] instead provides seman-tic similarity at the binary level, recompiling the generated function with the original compiler and flags and comparing the resulting machine code byte-for-byte against the original binary (wildcarding relocation sites). BW is the strictest metric in our suite since any divergence in instruction selection fails it, even between behaviorally identical programs and a pass certifies recovery up to compiler determinism. 

B. Experiments and Models 

We conduct three empirical evaluations, detailed as follows: 

First, “SOTA Method Evaluation” (reported in Section VI-A) evaluates the impact of the following: (1) Design choices of SOTA methods following the taxonomy defined in Section III; 

(2) Generalization of SOTA methods over four standard (x86-64, aarch64, riscv64, x86-32) and five embedded (armv7m, armv8m, armv7a, armv8a32, mips32le) architectures, five op-timization levels ( O0-O3, Os ), and stripped and unstripped symbol variants; and (3) Spearman correlation of the metrics suite derived from the above. We select LLM4Decompile (assembly and Ghidra) [64], SCCDEC [31], Nova [41], SK2Decompile [63], DeGPT [38], FidelityGPT [78], and Agent4Decompile [77] for evaluations, encompassing majority of the identified methodology axes (M , C, R, I, A) and their subcategories, subject to code availability. Only C 3 (agentic control-flow) has no representative method in our evalua-tion since Cao [16], which is the only identified agentic approach, has not published its code. We note again that Agent4Decompile [77] is classified as an iterative method. Methods that fine-tune or pre-train their own models are evaluated using their specified models, while methods that use off-the-shelf models are evaluated using HuggingFace sourced gemma4-31b-it [67] for comparison at the same intelligence capability. 

Second, “Taxonomy-driven Pipeline Ablation” (reported in Section VI-B) ablates five aspects of the source recovery pipeline: (1) Base input representation (hex, assembly, IR, pseudo-C) provided to a single-role, single-pass pipeline (SP), and subsequently; (2) Enrichment channels (callee imports, .rodata, .data/.bss, CFG, and frame ABI) that aid SP with additional context; (3) LLMs of different scale in the SP pipeline; (4) Iterative control-flow with one compiler diagnos-tic feedback iteration (CIT); and (5) Review-role (RR) pipeline using a reviewer LLM to improve the code based on static analysis. Each pipeline uses open-weight LLMs from Hug-gingFace: gemma4-12b-it [67], gemma4-31b-it [67] and qwen3.8-27b [52] at bf16 quantization, 0.1 tempera-ture, and a 8192-token generation limit and within a 32768-token context. For reference, a CIT or RR pipeline with a 31b LLM is represented as “CIT-31b” or “RR-31b”, respectively. Ablations are conducted on 50 C-code compiled on x86-64 at two optimizations (O0 and O2). 

Third, “Source Language Study” (reported in Section VI-C) uses the representation selected by RQ3 to test (1) same-language recovery, where the model is told which language (among C, C++, Go, Rust, Fortran and Ada) the binary was built from and must reproduce it, and (2) cross-language recovery, where it must produce behaviorally equivalent C from a non-C-derived binary. These tasks are conducted us-ing the SP and CIT pipelines, and gemma4-31b-it and 

qwen3.8-27b . C is selected as the cross-language target as it has the most mature tooling for downstream vulnerability and malware analysis.

C. Source Language Study RQ4 

Fig. 11 presents the RCI and RE of same- and cross-language recovery using SP-31b and CIT-31b pipelines for C, C++, Go, Rust, Fortran, and Ada. Same-language recovery exposes a performance gap when viewed through the metrics of compilability and functional similarity. With the exception of Ada (16% RCI), non-C languages recompile at rates com-parable to or better than C (C++ 94%, Fortran 85%, Rust 68%), yet their RE collapses to 1-13% compared to 47% for C. A single compiler iteration widens this split: CIT-31b drives RCI up to 94–100% C++, Go, Rust, and Fortran (94-100%) while RE improves from 7% 16% for Go and only by 1% for Rust. The failure-stage decomposition in Fig. 12a shows that 80–88% of C++ and Rust cases compile but cannot be called 

by the evaluation harness due to incorrect signatures. Fortran instead runs and diverges (82% I/O divergence) and Ada does not benefit from compilation repair. Cross-language cases present an interesting observation. Single-pass RCI is far lower (4–36%) because the model must strip the source language’s runtime scaffolding from the pseudo-C. That being the case, successful compilations over-whelmingly pass the I/O tests. Further, iteration repairs leads to nearly 2 × functional gains for Fortran (17% →29%), C++ (14% →27%), and Ada (8% →13%). Go and Rust see poor performance since their binaries embed goroutine scheduling, bounds-check panics, and garbage-collection machinery that the LLM is unable to translate into plain C. Fig. 12b shows that recovering a non-C binary into C beats recovering it into its own source language for four of the five languages (Fortran 29% vs. 11%, C++ 27% vs. 12%, Ada 13% vs. 1%, Rust 10% vs. 1%). Finally, all cases remain below the C →C ceiling (59% RE with iteration). To empirically validate the hypothesis that a performance drop occurs due to C-centric lifting by Ghidra, we evaluate the cross-language CIT-31b pipelines to ingest 

asm . Evidently, as seen in Fig. 10 and 12b, pseudo-C adds 10.4% RCI and 63.9% RE for recovery of C source from C-derived assembly, but loses 8.4% RCI and gains only 16.2% RE for non-C languages. However, the improvement in cross-language RE for non-C to C over asm is still a motivation to use classical decompiler pseudo-C for source recovery. We consolidate our research findings based on our research questions and share open problems where applicable: 1) Value of the metrics (RQ1): While Agent4Decompile leads the SOTA methods in terms of recompilability, functional similarity and byte-level semantics, Fideli-tyGPT and SK2Decompile outperform in terms of read-ability and lexical similarity. This observation highlights the necessity of our multi-dimensional evaluation. 2) Signature recovery failure (RQ1-4): Comparison of RC vs RCI across our evaluations shows that while the SOTA (and recent LLMs in general) are highly capable at generating compilable code, they fail more often in generating the correct signature, which can be mitigated with harness-aware compiler feedback iterations. 3) Pseudo-C as a cross-architecture portability layer (RQ3): Across the architectures, methods that ingest pseudo-C showcase higher fidelity compared to those that ingest assembly. Apparently, the uniformity of Ghidra’s output absorbs the architectural shift that x86-64-assembly-trained models are sensitive to. 4) Performance drops for Thumb2 ISA (RQ2): Even the best performing methods show worse performance on Thumb2 architectures such as armv7-m and armv8-m, compared to standard and non-Thumb2 embedded architectures. Since Thumb2 dominates deployed IoT firmware [66] where source recovery is an important as-pect of maintenance and upgrade of legacy firmware and vulnerability detection [42], the fidelity of LLM-assisted methods in embedded systems is an open direction. 5) Drop with optimization (RQ2): RE declines monoton-ically from O0-O3 , and remains comparable for Os and 

O2 since they share the same core functionality except code alignment and size-padding optimization [33]. 6) Off-the-shelf models and scale (RQ3): Larger LLMs achieve higher overall fidelity, and methods that use general-purpose, off-the-shelf LLMs match the perfor-mance of those fine-tuned on pseudo-C. As a future work, clear benefits of fine-tuned models may arise for non-C. 7) Control flow and multi-role benefits (RQ3): Iteration improves RCI, BW, and RE over single-pass while sac-rificing readability due to the scaffolding introduced to fix compilation errors. Multiple roles improve readability and lexical similarity, with marginal differences in com-pilability, semantic, and functional similarity. 8) Predominance of C (RQ4): The fidelity of source recov-ery is worse on non-C-derived binaries. Cross-recovery to C, however, is better than recovering the original source. Ghidra pseudo-C offers limited gain over assembly com-pared to same-language recovery to C. These observations point towards cross-language decompilers, training and augmentation as open directions. VIII. T HREATS TO VALIDITY 

ExeBench is a public benchmark and predates the training cutoffs of every model used in our work, and may be present in their pre-training dataset [29]. However, our focus is on the comparative performance between methods. We also note that recovery quality is sensitive to prompt wording, input format-ting, and decoding settings [29]. SOTA methods are compared using their specified prompts, while ablations fix one prompt per task. We did not search over paraphrases, and different phrasings would likely move absolute scores. That being said, we publish all prompts along with source code. Lastly, we assume the availability and derivability of the function call interface due to the general availability of repository-level context in codebases. However, compiler interactions under the assumption of no repository-level context will show limited improvement in RCI. IX. C ONCLUSION 

This work presented the first SoK dedicated to LLM-assisted source code recovery. We created a novel design taxonomy and organized the published methods along five axes. We also consolidated the fragmented metric landscape in this topic into six key dimensions for a holistic evalua-tion. We evaluated representative SOTA methods on 45,000 samples built from 500 functions, 9 standard and embedded architectures, 5 optimization levels, and 2 symbol variants. Further, we ran a controlled, taxonomy-driven ablation of input representation, contextual enrichment, model scale, iterative feedback, and review roles, deriving key insights on impactful design choices. We also measured same- and cross-language (to C) recovery across mature and legacy source languages. Open problems for future work include the general fidelity, signature failures, and coverage of non-C and Thumb2-ISA. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

We make the following considerations: (1) While source recovery aids malware analysis, firmware vulnerability as-sessment, and legacy maintenance, it may also be used for IP theft and license circumvention. Our work measures and bounds the limitations of source recovery, and proposes no new method for the same. However, our evaluation harness may be used by people with malicious intent, and therefore, we urge readers to use our code ethically. (2) All our evaluations were conducted using permissively licensed C (Exebench). (3) All SOTA method evaluations were conducted using their original publicly sourced code from their respective authors. All deviations have been documented in the Appendix and our results were conducted on a unified evaluation set to ensure fair comparison. LLM U SAGE CONSIDERATIONS 

LLMs were used for editorial purposes in this manuscript, and all outputs were inspected by the authors to ensure accuracy and originality. We used Anthropic’s Claude Opus-5 and Fable-5.1, to assist with writing the code. The cross-language translations were done using Claude Fable-5.1, val-idated against the I/O test of the original C function for functional symmetry, and spot checked by the authors. All numerical results and figures were produced by our own pipeline, generated directly from the raw experimental outputs and checked against the underlying data. The authors have reviewed all AI-assisted code, and take full responsibility for the accuracy, originality, and integrity of this work. Further, LLMs play an integral role in our experiments since they are the focus of our evaluations and thus unavoidable to use. However, we carefully considered the extent of experiments required to support our claims: the SOTA evaluations study used a larger evaluation set for fair comparison across pub-lished methods; downstream ablations (RQ3/4) used only a subset (50 functions per language on one architecture and two optimizations) to derive their findings. Thus, our LLM usage has been under the overarching consideration of accountability, correctness, transparency, and responsibility. OPEN SCIENCE
