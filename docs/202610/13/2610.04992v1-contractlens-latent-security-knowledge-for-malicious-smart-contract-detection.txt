## ABSTRACT 

Detecting malicious smart contracts is essential to safeguarding the Web3.0 ecosystem. However, existing auditing methods based on large language mod-els (LLMs) largely rely on prompt engineering or attack-specific fine-tuning, while the internal representations that support malicious logic detection remain poorly characterized. To address this gap, we propose ContractLens, a framework that localizes and uses latent security knowledge in pretrained LLMs for inter-pretable malicious smart contract detection. Specifically, we first use layerwise linear probes to identify candidate layers that distinguish malicious from benign contracts, then select critical layers based on their sensitivity to malicious logic removal and stability under behaviour-preserving surface modifications. Next, within these critical layers, we combine activation contrast, malicious-logic speci-ficity, and probe weights into a tri-factor importance score and adaptively se-lect malicious contract-discriminative units (MCDUs) using the knee point of the ranked score curve. Finally, we concatenate the selected units’ activations into a compact representation and train a lightweight classifier for detection, keep-ing the pretrained model frozen throughout. Neuron masking experiments show that masking MCDUs causes larger drops in detection performance than masking randomly selected neurons or neurons matched in activation magnitude. Con-tractLens achieves higher average detection accuracy than the evaluated methods on all three pretrained models. Further evaluations demonstrate generalization to unseen malicious intents and show that, with the identified MCDUs fixed, a classi-fier trained on few labelled samples can still achieve strong detection performance. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Smart contracts (Zheng et al., 2020) manage digital assets by executing predefined rules and form core infrastructure for decentralized finance (Chen & Bellavitis, 2020), digital asset trading, and on-chain governance. However, financial fraud and other malicious activities continue to threaten this ecosystem (Miao et al., 2025; 2026). Because deployed contract is typically difficult to modify di-rectly, hidden malicious logic may continue to threaten users’ assets during subsequent interactions. Accurately detecting such logic is therefore important for safeguarding the Web3.0 ecosystem. Existing smart contract security analysis follows three main technical routes: program analysis, su-pervised learning, and auditing with large language models (LLMs). Tools such as Oyente, Securify, and Slither detect violations of specified security properties through symbolic execution, dependency analysis, or static analysis (Badruddoja et al., 2021; Tsankov et al., 2018; Feist et al., 2019), with their coverage depending on the properties and rules encoded in the analyzer. Supervised methods such as SmartIntentNN and its successor (Huang et al., 2025b; 2026) extend detection to malicious intent by learning mappings from contract representations to intent labels. More recently, LLM-based approaches (Wei et al., 2024b; Sun et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2025; Jie et al., 2025) use prompt design, external knowledge, and agent workflows to elicit code understanding and vulnerability rea-soning. We draw on experienced developers’ ability to flag a purported refund function that redirects funds to the contract controller as suspicious before identify-ing the attack type. Their judgment hinges on the mismatch between the function’s stated purpose and its actual effect. This motivates our hypothesis that LLMs pretrained on smart contract code may en-code similar security-relevant distinctions. To test this hypothesis, we must determine whether the rel-evant internal signals reflect malicious logic rather than superficial code correlations, and whether they contribute to detection. We therefore seek represen-tations that change when malicious logic is removed but remain stable under behaviour-preserving edits, then assess their functional contribution through neu-ron interventions. Building on this hypothesis, we propose ContractLens, a framework that localizes and uses latent security knowledge in pretrained LLMs for interpretable malicious smart contract detection. We first train layerwise linear probes on the hidden representations of a frozen LLM to identify candidate layers that distinguish malicious from benign contracts. Among these candidates, we select critical layers by testing sensitivity to malicious logic removal and stability under behaviour-preserving sur-face modifications. Next, within the multilayer perceptron (MLP) modules of these layers, we com-bine activation contrast, malicious-logic specificity, and probe weights into a tri-factor importance score and adaptively select malicious contract-discriminative units (MCDUs) using the knee point of the ranked score curve. Finally, we concatenate MCDUs activations into a compact representa-tion and train a lightweight classifier for detection, keeping the pretrained model frozen throughout. Neuron masking experiments show that masking MCDUs causes larger drops in detection perfor-mance than masking randomly selected neurons or neurons matched in activation magnitude. The main contributions are summarized as follows: • We propose ContractLens, an interpretable framework that identifies critical layers and neurons in frozen pretrained LLMs and uses their latent security knowledge for malicious smart contract detection. • We identify MCDUs through tri-factor scoring and adaptive selection, showing that their activations enable effective detection and that masking them causes larger performance drops than matched neuron controls. • Experiments show higher average detection accuracy than evaluated baselines across three models, generalization to unseen malicious intents, and effective classifier training with few labelled samples using fixed MCDUs. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

LLM-based Smart Contract Security Analysis. Program-analysis tools detect violations of spec-ified security properties in smart contracts (Badruddoja et al., 2021; Tsankov et al., 2018). LLM-based auditing complements these approaches by using code understanding and semantic reasoning to inspect contract behaviour. LLM-SmartAudit employs specialized agents and structured analysis prompts (Wei et al., 2025), while LLM4Vuln systematically evaluates the effects of external vulner-ability knowledge, program context, and prompt design (Sun et al., 2024). SmartGuard combines retrieved code examples with chain-of-thought reasoning for in-context learning (Ding et al., 2025), and Agent4Vul integrates LLM-derived semantic information with structural program representa-tions (Jie et al., 2025). Further work explores business-invariant-guided contrastive auditing (Gao et al., 2026), knowledge distillation into lightweight auditing models (Wei et al., 2026), and domain-adapted code representations for unsafe intent detection (Huang et al., 2025c). Internal Representations and Neuron-level Interpretability. Mechanistic interpretability investi-gates how information is represented and used within language models (Bereska & Gavves, 2024). Linear probes reveal information decodable from intermediate representations, but predictive accu-racy alone does not establish its functional relevance (Somvanshi et al., 2026). Neuron-level attri-bution localizes units associated with factual knowledge within feed-forward networks (FFNs) (Dai et al., 2022). Skill-neuron and sparse-probing studies (Wang et al., 2022; Gurnee et al., 2023) fur-ther identify small sets of task-relevant units and assess their roles through targeted interventions. In code security, recent work (Dao et al., 2026) localizes FFN neurons associated with malicious Python package detection and evaluates their effects through neuron interventions. Complementary work on explainable smart contract vulnerability detection provides evidence through expert-defined patterns (Liu et al., 2021), code regions (Wang et al., 2026), and explanatory graph substructures (Yu et al., 2026). However, identifying pretrained LLM components encoding malicious contract logic, controlling for surface cues, and verifying their contribution to detection remain underexplored. 

## 3 METHOD 

As shown in Figure 2, ContractLens localizes and extracts latent security knowledge from pretrained LLMs in two steps. First, it evaluates each layer for malicious contract separability, malicious logic sensitivity, and surface perturbation stability, retaining only layers that satisfy all three criteria as critical layers. Second, within the MLP modules of these critical layers, it identifies key neurons through tri-factor importance scoring and curvature-guided adaptive truncation. We refer to these neurons as MCDUs and use them for malicious smart contract detection. 3.1 CRITICAL LAYER LOCALIZATION

For binary detection, contracts containing at least one target intent are treated as malicious, resulting in 21,166 malicious and 10,975 benign contracts. This code-level label indicates the presence of latent ma-licious capabilities rather than confirmed on-chain exploitation. Accordingly, 21,166 contracts are labelled as malicious, while the remaining 10,975 contracts are labelled as benign. For critical-layer localization, we construct a balanced probing set by sampling 1,000 contracts annotated with at least one target intent and 1,000 benign contracts. For fine-grained localization, we identify MCDUs separately for each target intent by formulating ten independent one-vs-benign binary classification tasks. For each task, contracts annotated with the target intent serve as positive samples, while benign contracts serve as negative samples. To prevent data leakage, we group contracts sharing the same creator or source code and assign each group entirely to one dataset partition. For each intent, we split the resulting groups into training, validation, and test sets using a ratio of 0.64 : 0 .16 : 0 .20 .

Metrics. Following Zheng et al. (2020), we evaluate classification performance using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and accuracy (ACC), and the F1-score. 

Baselines. We first assess the ability of different LLMs to detect malicious smart contracts by pro-viding contract code directly in the prompt, and use these results as the default baseline. These include one general-purpose LLM, Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), and two code-oriented LLMs, Qwen2.5-Coder-7B (Hui et al., 2024) and CodeGemma-2.5-7B (Team et al., 2024). Then, we also compare our method with seven representative smart contract detectors. EDSCVD (Wu et al., 2024) integrates adversarial sample augmentation with dual-channel feature extraction. FTSmartAu-dit (Wei et al., 2024a) fine-tunes compact LLMs using knowledge distilled from stronger models for smart contract auditing. DBESN (Bedi et al., 2025) uses a dual-branch Siamese network to capture discriminative contract features. SCMDetector (Huang et al., 2025a) combines language-model-based representations with an attention-enhanced BiLSTM (Siami-Namini et al., 2019). SmartIn-6Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027 tentNN (Huang et al., 2025b) combines contextual sentence encoding, intent-feature highlighting, and a BiLSTM classifier. SmartIntentV2 (Huang et al., 2026) employs a domain-adapted pretrained model to learn smart contract representations. CodeXplain (Chen et al., 2026) combines LLM-generated code explanations with contract code to train a supervised vulnerability detector. 

Hyperparameters. We set the maximum input length to 2,048 tokens and process longer contracts with sliding windows overlapping by 512 tokens. We obtain contract-level representations by mean-pooling the non-padding token embeddings. ## 5 CONCLUSION 

This work investigates whether pretrained LLMs contain localized latent security knowledge that supports malicious smart contract detection without adapting the backbone. Our method localizes this knowledge at both the layer and neuron levels and uses the activations of the identified MC-DUs as compact features for a lightweight classifier. Across three pretrained models, our method achieves higher average detection accuracy than all evaluated methods while keeping the backbone frozen. Masking MCDUs causes larger performance drops than masking random or activation-matched neurons, supporting their functional specificity. Our method also generalizes to unseen malicious intents and maintains strong detection performance with limited labelled data. Together, these results demonstrate that latent security knowledge in pretrained LLMs can be systematically localized and utilized for interpretable and data-efficient malicious smart contract detection. 10 Published as a conference paper at ICLR 2027

## D MALICIOUS LOGIC REMOVAL AND SURFACE PERTURBATION 

Table 6 illustrates the two controlled code transformations used for critical layer localization. The original input x contains an owner-controlled transfer restriction associated with the honeypot in-tent. Malicious logic removal produces the counterfactual input xcf by removing this restriction, changing the smart contract from malicious to benign. In contrast, a behaviour-preserving surface perturbation produces xT = T (x) by renaming identifiers without changing the underlying unsafe logic or its label. These transformations separate malicious semantics from superficial code form. A representation associated with malicious logic should change substantially between x and xcf , while remaining stable between x and xT . This example explains why critical layer localization jointly evaluates malicious logic sensitivity and surface perturbation stability.

## F MORE DISCUSSIONS 

▷ Do MCDUs capture latent security knowledge or merely classification correlations? 

High classification performance alone does not establish that the identified units encode malicious logic, since a detector may rely on superficial cues such as identifiers, comments, or code tem-plates. ContractLens therefore introduces semantic controls at both the layer and neuron levels. We require the selected representations to respond to malicious-logic removal while remaining sta-ble under behaviour-preserving surface perturbations. In addition, masking the identified MCDUs causes substantially larger performance degradation than masking randomly selected or activation-matched neurons. Together, these results suggest that MCDUs capture functionally relevant signals associated with malicious contract semantics rather than arbitrary classification correlations.

▷ Can the identified security knowledge generalize beyond malicious behaviours observed during training? 

Our experiments indicate that the identified representations exhibit meaningful, although not uni-versal, cross-behaviour generalization. In the leave-one-intent-out evaluation, all localization and training stages exclude the held-out malicious intent, which is used only for testing. ContractLens still achieves higher average performance than the evaluated baselines. We further evaluate con-tracts deployed after the release of the backbone model and observe strong detection performance on several malicious mechanisms. These results suggest that the localized representations capture reusable characteristics of malicious contract logic rather than only memorizing individual attack categories. Nevertheless, ContractLens should not be interpreted as a universal detector for arbitrary future attacks, and its coverage remains dependent on the security knowledge represented by the underlying pretrained model. 19
