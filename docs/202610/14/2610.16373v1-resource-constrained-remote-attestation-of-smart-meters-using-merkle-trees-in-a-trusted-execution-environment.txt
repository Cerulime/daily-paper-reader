A B S T R A C T 

Smart meters are pivotal to the functioning of smart grids, as they measure and transmit energy consumption data that includes sensitive information about household energy usage patterns. While this data offers significant financial benefits, it can also reveal detailed insights into residents’ daily routines, making the security of smart meters a critical concern. This paper introduces a novel remote attestation method for smart meters that ensures the integrity and privacy of data exchanged between smart meters and central servers while detecting unauthorized tampering. Our approach structures the communication data into a Merkle Tree—a hash data structure that facilitates efficient data integrity and authenticity verification. All operations involved in constructing the Merkle root and hashing critical data are executed in the secure environment of a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) using OP-TEE, thereby maintaining the integrity and security of the smart meter’s data. Comparing our experimental results with the related works highlights the superiority of our method in terms of resource efficiency, security robustness, and the feasibility of implementation on smart meters and resource-constrained devices, thereby enhancing the overall security and reliability of smart grid systems. 

## 1. Introduction 

Smart meters play a crucial role in the functionality of a smart grid; without them, the seamless flow of the smart grid chain is disrupted. In addition to their primary function of calculating and measuring electricity consumption, smart meters serve as multifunctional devices that also collect and store important data, including time-of-use information, voltage levels, power quality metrics, fault diagnostics, and billing details [17]. The data collected by smart meters is important and sensitive and is essential to users’ privacy. Access to this in-formation by unauthorized people carries the risk of misuse and other potential dangers. For example, this data can reveal when people are away from home or asleep, which under-lines the importance of protecting and managing sensitive information responsibly. Smart meters can be connected to the smart grid in var-ious ways, e.g., via a wired network of power lines or wire-less communication technologies. Each of these methods is subject to certain communication standards that enable in-teraction with servers. Given the central role of smart meters in the smart grid, ensuring the security of these meters has become an integral aspect of the entire grid [13]. Various methods are used to protect the integrity of smart meters, in-cluding encryption, authentication, access control, intrusion detection, prevention systems, and data anonymization. ## 2. Background 

This section provides background information on key topics discussed in the paper, including the smart grid, types of smart meters, trusted execution environments, remote attestation, and Merkle Trees, to provide the required back-ground knowledge. 

2.1. Smart Grid 

The smart grid promotes bidirectional communication between electricity suppliers and their customers and en-ables the optimal integration of new energy solutions, such as solar and wind energy, into the existing infrastructure [15]. By integrating smart household meters into the smart grid, households can manage their electricity consumption effi-ciently [16]. Smart grids optimize energy distribution and manage-ment through components like the dispatching center, which oversees generation and distribution while analyzing smart meter data. Connectors and data aggregators facilitate seam-less communication and integration across the grid, enabling efficient operations, especially in smart homes. While smart meters enhance real-time monitoring and management, they raise security concerns, highlighting the need for secure communication. Together, these elements ensure efficient and reliable energy management in modern smart grids. A major difficulty with smart grids is the complexity of utilizing different protocols and functions for the connected end devices, including smart meters and concentrators [11]. This complexity leads to vulnerabilities that require ex-tensive source code to implement complicated systems. In addition, devices connected to home networks are inherently more vulnerable to attack than devices connected directly to controlled networks [27]. The main issue with smart meters is their vulnerability to cyber-attacks, which can lead to unauthorized access, power disruptions, and potential network damage. On the other hand, ensuring software integrity protects the firmware from unauthorized changes, while data integrity ensures the accuracy and consistency of transmitted information. Robust encryption, regular updates, and secure communications are critical to mitigate these risks, especially as smart meters are increasingly integrated into the broader Internet of Things (IoT) ecosystem [21]. In the broader landscape of smart management systems, modern distribution grids employ a range of metering and monitoring devices that can be categorized into different

These devices are substantially changing the grid landscape, with four main categories identified: smart energy sensors, smart feed-in meters, smart distribution transformer monitoring devices, and remote control switches. This discussion will focus primarily on the importance of smart energy sensors and smart meters, highlighting their critical role in the evolving energy management ecosystem [7]. 

2.2. Smart meter 

Electricity metering is critical to the operation of electri-cal systems and plays an important role in energy manage-ment, energy billing, and ensuring the reliability and safety of the system [20]. With the development of smart grids and the growing need to improve electricity management and maximize energy efficiency, the deployment of smart meters has become increasingly important [28]. Smart meters, an evolution of conventional energy me-ters, have new features designed to provide innovative solu-tions, particularly in the collection of relevant information on electricity consumption and two-way communication, allowing them to interact dynamically with centralized sys-tems [6]. These features facilitate remote disconnection and reconnection of electrical loads, as well as the monitoring and controlling of user devices in the context of smart buildings [9]. The integration of various sensors and control devices, supported by particular communication infrastructures, un-derlines the versatile role of smart meters in efficiently managing energy demand and loads [29]. In addition, smart meters play a central role in supporting dynamic pricing mechanisms and facilitating the bi-directional flow of elec-tricity between residential units (or microgrids) and the gen-eral electricity grid. However, the widespread deployment of smart meters raises significant privacy concerns as they can collect comprehensive data on household activities [22]. While smart meters have numerous potential benefits for consumers and utilities, there are also significant concerns about security and privacy risks. Primary concerns include unauthorized access to data, remote control vulnerabilities, and inherent security and privacy flaws [4, 6]. In order to mitigate these risks, robust security measures need to be implemented, such as strong encryption, strict access controls, increased public awareness, and the creation of a strong legal framework. Addressing these concerns is crucial for broad acceptance and trust in smart meter technology. Ultimately, the decision to roll out smart meters is influenced by assessing the perceived benefits and potential risks [19, 20]. 

2.3. Trusted Execution Environment 

The Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) is an evolv-ing technology that improves the security of computer sys-tems by creating a secure and isolated environment for the execution of sensitive applications. TEE is often referred to as a secure environment or trust zone and protects the code and data of active applications from unauthorized access, tampering, and intrusion. The rapid growth of the Internet of Things requires intelligent systems that often run on ARM-based proces-sors [10]. To improve software security, ARM has developed TrustZone technology, which creates a foundation of trust by dividing resources into secure and insecure parts. Despite the presence of untrusted third parties, TrustZone effectively secures high-quality data and enables a reliable execution environment [25]. ARM Cortex-M23 and Cortex-M33 based microcon-trollers have emerged as the dominant choice for resource-constrained IoT deployments, combining hardware-enforced TrustZone-M with ultra-low power consumption and a com-petitive price; devices such as the Microchip SAM L11, Nuvoton NuMicro M2351, and NXP LPC55S6x illustrate this trend across different vendors, each delivering secure boot, isolated execution, and real-time performance on a single low-cost chip. Smart meters represent one of the most practical and cost-sensitive deployments of this tech-nology, where the economics of scale make every per-unit decision consequential: the STMicroelectronics STM32L5, a Cortex-M33 running at 110 MHz with 512 KB Flash and 256 KB SRAM, exemplifies how TrustZone-M can be put to work in exactly this context, integrating the TEE directly on-chip to eliminate the need for a discrete Secure Element or HSM while drawing remarkably little power and process—a combination well-suited to battery-assisted or energy-harvesting meter designs. Within that isolated environment, the meter’s most sensitive operations—energy measurement, billing computation, cryptographic key man-agement, and secure over-the-air firmware updates—execute entirely apart from the untrusted application world, pre-serving both integrity and confidentiality without dedicated security hardware, and across a national rollout of millions of units, even a modest reduction in per-unit bill-of-materials cost can translate to substantial aggregate savings, making this class of ARM-based TEE architecture not only techni-cally adequate but economically compelling for large-scale infrastructures. 

2.4. Remote attestation 

Remote attestation is a cybersecurity process that can be used to verify the trustworthiness of systems or appli-cations on other devices by digitally verifying identity and integrity. It is crucial for confidential data processing and ensures that the devices comply with the expected settings. Key components include the verification server (verifier), the verification client (credential sender), the key broker service (KBS), which stores secrets and releases them after verification, and the key broker client (receives keys from the KBS). Remote attestation enables dynamic responses to vulnerabilities and more comprehensive policy authentica-tion compared to Trusted Platform Modules (TPM), which are essential for secure data processing. 

2.5. Merkle Tree 

Merkle Trees are cryptographic data structures that en-hance the efficiency and security of data validation and storage. They use a root hash derived from individual data

This structure reduces storage requirements, supports parallel verification, and is ideal for secure, distributed sys-tems requiring efficient integrity checks and tamper detec-tion. Merkle Trees excel in secure outsourced data sys-tems by enabling effective updates and verification. They construct a tree of cryptographic hashes, combining child node hashes into a single root hash, which is signed by the data owner. Each update generates a new root hash that ensures data integrity, making Merkle Trees well-suited for applications like secure key-value outsourcing [8]. 

## 3. Related Works 

This section provides an overview of prior research on smart grid technologies, with a particular focus on smart meters and their associated security challenges. The anal-ysis critically examines existing methods for securing smart meters and contextualizes them against the novel approach proposed in this paper. A range of methodologies has been explored to en-hance the security of smart meters, with special attention to safeguarding user data against potential breaches. Central to these efforts are mechanisms that address core security principles, including authentication, authorization, data pro-tection, privacy, and regulatory compliance. Through these mechanisms, researchers aim to mitigate vulnerabilities that could compromise the integrity of smart grid systems. Fur-thermore, our review highlights how utility companies can leverage robust security frameworks to protect sensitive information, ensure system reliability, and foster consumer trust. Emphasis is placed on the comparative strengths and limitations of existing techniques, laying the groundwork for our proposed. 

3.1. Authentication and Authorization 

The integration of compression and authentication in advanced metering infrastructure (AMI) addresses key chal-lenges in managing smart meter data efficiently and se-curely [18]. Prior research has explored separate domains of compression and authentication. Compression techniques, such as wavelet-based methods, SVD, and compressive sens-ing (CS), reduce data volume and processing demands, while authentication mechanisms like cryptographic methods and physical-layer approaches ensure data integrity. However, cryptographic methods are computationally intensive, and lightweight physical-layer techniques may fall short against closely matched intrusion signals. The authors propose a unified CS-based framework for simultaneous compression and authentication. It employs a shared measurement matrix as a secret key and residual error-based hypothesis testing, achieving high energy efficiency, reduced computational complexity, and low authentication error rates under prac-tical conditions. The approach is particularly suited for low-cost smart meters. Nevertheless, challenges include potential fidelity loss due to lossy compression, limited adaptability to diverse attack scenarios, and complexity in real-world deployment [18]. The implementation of smart grids and AMI has driven significant research into secure communication frameworks, particularly in key management and authentication systems. Among these, the logical key hierarchy (LKH) mecha-nism [1] stands out as a robust approach for scalable and efficient key management in dynamic SG environments. LKH employs a tree-based structure where each user main-tains a copy of the keys for their leaf and all nodes leading to the root. This hierarchical arrangement enables secure key distribution for large-scale demand response projects, significantly reducing storage and communication costs. Furthermore, the method allows multiple projects to share new key sets, enhancing scalability and adaptability in rapidly changing environments. LKH ensures that the rekey-ing process for users joining or leaving a group does not affect the overall performance of other nodes by optimizing rekeying operations through a multigroup key graph struc-ture. However, the mechanism is not without limitations. In cases of node compromise, the multicast group rekeying process can become resource-intensive, requiring a delicate balance between the number of transmissions and storage. Another noteworthy advancement is the integration of physically unclonable functions (PUFs) in hardware-oriented authentication systems [2]. PUFs exploit the intrinsic ran-domness in hardware manufacturing to generate unique cryptographic keys, providing a robust defense against repli-cation and impersonation attacks. Recent research has ex-tended this concept by combining PUFs with identity-based authentication, creating a hybrid framework that leverages both symmetric cryptography and identity-based systems. For example, PUF-based frameworks eliminate the need for additional volatile memory systems, as secret keys are derived from hardware properties instead of being stored. This approach has proven effective in mitigating denial-of-service (DoS) attacks and reducing communication latency by optimizing key management at the hardware level. Ad-ditionally, the integration of PUFs with Hamming code has demonstrated reduced overhead in data transmission while maintaining strong security. However, challenges persist in terms of real-time computational demands and the com-plexity of integrating PUFs into existing AMI systems. The cost and technical requirements for deploying PUF-based systems, particularly in low-cost smart meters, remain areas for further exploration. Logical Key Hierarchy (LKH) [1] and PUF-based sys-tems [2] demonstrate the strengths and weaknesses of con-temporary advancements in secure communication for SGs. LKH offers better scalability, efficient key distribution, and reduced storage requirements, making it suitable for dy-namic environments with diverse user demands. However, it faces challenges in handling resource-intensive rekeying processes during node compromise. On the other hand, PUF-based systems ensure enhanced security, robust resistance to impersonation attacks, and reduced data transmission overhead but face integration challenges due to high costs

3.2. Data Integrity and Confidentiality 

The cloud-edge-end architecture [12] is integrated edge computing for localized data processing, reducing the re-liance on centralized master stations and improving system responsiveness. This architecture uses IoT proxy terminals at the edge layer to dynamically manage smart meters through a plug-and-play model, enabling low-latency, scalable, and secure device integration. However, potential security risks arise if edge nodes are compromised, and deployment into existing AMI systems poses infrastructure challenges. An-other contribution is the use of fully homomorphic encryp-tion (FHE) combined with a lightweight packet reorganiza-tion protocol, enhancing data security during aggregation and reducing packet loss through hash-based mechanisms and sliding address windows. In this regard, the cloud-edge-end architecture [12] achieves high bandwidth utilization and low latency while allowing secure mathematical oper-ations on encrypted data. Despite its benefits, the computa-tional overhead of FHE and the complexity of large-scale deployment present challenges for low-cost smart meters, underscoring the need for further optimization in real-world implementations. Another important contribution to the domain of privacy and efficiency in AMI is the Efficient and Privacy-Preserving Data Collection and Access Control (EPDA) [3] scheme. It is designed for multi-recipient AMI networks, addressing the limitations of single-recipient schemes. This framework employs a novel proxy re-encryption mechanism to facili-tate data aggregation prior to re-encryption. It ensures that aggregated power consumption data (APCD) is accessible only to intended recipients while maintaining fine-grained access control. EPDA achieves low computational and com-munication overhead by combining lightweight encryption and re-encryption techniques. This approach also prevents disclosing customers’ distribution to unauthorized entities, ensuring fair electricity trading. Moreover, EPDA resists collusion attacks between malicious entities, such as distri-bution network operators (DNOs) and the data communica-tion company (DCC). However, integrating EPDA into ex-isting systems may require substantial infrastructure changes and complex key management mechanisms. 

3.3. Privacy Considerations 

Energy theft remains a significant challenge in smart grids, causing substantial economic losses. A notable ap-proach is employing peer-to-peer (P2P) computing for privacy-preserving energy theft detection [23]. This method lever-ages distributed algorithms solving linear systems, such as LU and QR decompositions, to identify fraudulent users

without requiring users to disclose their private energy consumption data. The P2P framework ensures user privacy by allowing smart meters to compute honesty coefficients 

collaboratively, preserving individual data integrity. It offers robust privacy protection and scalable theft detection across networks of various sizes. However, challenges include numerical instability in large-scale networks with LU de-composition. The state estimation-based energy theft detection frame-work [24] employs Kalman filters to identify anomalies in microgrid systems. The privacy-preserving bias estimation algorithm (PPBE) extends this by decoupling bias estima-tion from system state estimation, preserving users’ privacy while efficiently identifying theft. The method separates the computation into two parallel filters: one estimates unbiased system states while the other computes biases using resid-uals. This decomposition ensures faster convergence and numerical stability. The advantages of this approach include robust detection capabilities, scalability in large microgrid networks, and strong privacy guarantees through decentral-ized computation. However, the computational complexity of the filters and reliance on accurate system parameters pose challenges for real-world deployment. In conclusion, our review of existing smart meter secu-rity techniques underscores the multifaceted challenges and advancements within the field. Various approaches, includ-ing unified compressive sensing frameworks [18], Logical Key Hierarchy (LKH) [1], Physically Unclonable Func-tions (PUFs) [2], cloud-edge-end architectures [12], and privacy-preserving data aggregation schemes like EPDA [3], demonstrate significant strides in enhancing authentication, authorization, data integrity, and privacy in smart grids. These solutions offer benefits such as improved scalability, attack resistance, and efficient data management, yet they also present limitations, including computational overhead, integration complexities, and vulnerability to specific attack vectors. Additionally, innovative techniques in energy theft detection, leveraging peer-to-peer computing [23] and state estimation-based frameworks [24], highlight the ongoing efforts to balance privacy with effective anomaly detection. As illustrated in Table 1, each method presents a unique set of advantages and challenges, providing a clear compari-son that informs the development of more robust security solutions. This critical analysis highlights the strengths and weaknesses of current technologies, providing a foundation for our proposed method to address existing gaps and en-hance the security and reliability of smart grid systems. 

## 4. Proposed Method 

In this section, we present our work to improve the security of smart meters in smart homes. Figure 2 illustrates an overview of the communication between servers and smart meters. Before a smart meter can be integrated into the grid or verify its identity to the server before transmitting critical data, it must go through a series of steps. In this process, the smart meter’s initial and sensitive data, which are static and serve as the leaves of a Merkle Tree, are used to generate the root of the tree. This root is then combined with a random Nonce and hashed as part of the remote attestation process. The resulting data is transmitted to the server, which compares the received data to its stored data for validation. If the remote attestation is successful, the smart meter is authorized to transmit data. If it fails, the smart meter is blacklisted by the server, and its data transmission is blocked. During this process, both predictable and unpredictable events can occur, such as power outages or loss of communi-cation between meters or with the server. In scenarios where smart meters are responsible for transmitting electricity us-age data, the system ensures that any data collected during power or communication outages is securely transmitted to the appropriate systems and servers once connectivity is restored. In order to support this functionality, the smart meter architecture is equipped with a backup battery and temporary memory to retain data during interruptions, en-suring data integrity and continuous operation within the smart grid. This approach enhances data transmission reliability and security within the smart grid while ensuring system re-silience against potential disruptions, making it a robust solution for large-scale smart meter deployments. With the proliferation of smart homes, smart meters have proven to be critical components that generate and process important data, making them potential targets for cyberat-tacks. To mitigate this risk, we have integrated advanced se-curity features into the smart meter, utilizing secure hashing to protect data integrity and significantly delay unauthorized access attempts. Our proposed method involves constructing a Merkle Tree to organize and securely validate the original raw smart meter data. These components, essential for the accurate and safe operation of the smart meter, form the leaves of the Merkle Tree. Additionally, a symmetric key is embedded on the smart meter’s board during manufacturing, ensuring that only the smart meter and the server possess this key. This symmetric key is integrated into the hash operations between the meter and the server, enhancing the overall security by enabling secure and authenticated communication channels. The root of the tree is transferred to a remote server after hashing for verification to ensure the integrity and authen-ticity of the smart meter data before it is integrated into the smart grid. All steps, including the formation of Merkle Tree and initial data management, are executed securely within a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE), providing a robust data protection framework.

The primary data of a smart meter includes the device’s unique identifier, initial security information, basic config-urations, manufacturer information, the installed software version, the initial energy consumption profile, environ-mental data, communication parameters, and the history of activities and initial settings. This data, which is essential for the correct and safe operation of the meter before it is connected to the smart grid, forms the leaves of the Merkle Tree. After the data has been aggregated and hashed and the tree and its root have been formed, a remote attestation procedure is performed to ensure the integrity and security of the data transmitted between the meter and the server. The remote attestation is performed by first calculating the root of Merkle Tree, which contains the meter’s sensitive data, and then concatenating it with a Nonce. This concatenation results in a unique and unpredictable value, further pro-cessed using the HMAC algorithm and the initially shared symmetric key to generate a secure and unique hash value. This hash value is then sent to the server. The server performs the same operations with the cor-responding data and compares the received hash with the hash stored in its system. The match between the two hashes means the meter and its data are intact and valid, and the remote attestation has been successfully completed so that the meter can join the smart grid. However, the mismatch be-tween the received and calculated hashes indicates a possible problem in the meter firmware or manipulation of the data, resulting in the failure of the remote attestation. In this case, the meter must not be connected to the network, and further investigation is required to identify and rectify the problem or detect possible threats. If the remote attestation fails, the process is repeated to check whether there is a temporary problem. If the error persists, the meter will be blacklisted for further investigation. Remote attestation is not only used to initially verify smart meters, but it also plays a critical role when updating smart meters and before transmitting critical meter data. Remote attestation is performed before an update begins. This ensures that the meter operates in a factory setting and remains valid and free of unauthorized modifications. Once the attestation is successfully completed, the updated data is sent from the server to the smart meter. Upon completion of the update process, another remote attestation is performed to confirm that the updated meter has been properly in-tegrated into the system and that secure communications have been established. These steps ensure that the meter will continue to function in a secure manner within the smart grid after the update. The smart meter also uses remote attestation to verify the integrity of the outgoing information before transmitting sensitive data. Only after successful attestation is the data transferred to the server. This is a critical step in ensuring that only valid and secure information is transferred to the server, thus maintaining the security of the entire data transfer process.

4.4. Key Management 

Effective key management is central to the security guar-antees of the proposed architecture. Given the resource-constrained nature of the smart meter platform—an ARM Cortex-M33 based microcontroller with limited processing power, memory, and energy—the design deliberately relies on symmetric key cryptography rather than asymmetric (public-key) schemes for protecting communication between the meter and the Distribution Management System (DMS). 

4.4.1. Rationale for Symmetric Key Cryptography 

Asymmetric cryptographic algorithms, such as RSA or elliptic-curve schemes, typically require significantly larger key sizes, more complex arithmetic operations, and greater memory footprint for both code and intermediate computa-tion. On a resource-constrained embedded device such as the smart meter used in this work, these requirements translate directly into higher energy consumption, increased latency per cryptographic operation, and additional RAM overhead that competes with the already limited resources available inside the Secure World. By contrast, symmetric key cryptography—and in par-ticular HMAC-based constructions—offers substantially lower computational cost, smaller code size, and faster execution, all of which are critical for a device that must perform frequent metering, billing, and attestation operations un-der tight power and memory constraints. This efficiency advantage, combined with the fact that the TEE already provides a hardware-isolated environment for secure key storage (eliminating much of the motivation for public-key based key exchange), makes symmetric cryptography the more practical and economically justified choice for this class of critical devices. 

4.4.2. Secure Key Storage within the TEE 

Rather than relying on an external Secure Element (SE) or Hardware Security Module (HSM), the symmetric key used by each meter is generated and stored entirely within the Secure Storage component of the on-chip Trusted Execution Environment (TrustZone-M Secure World). Because this key resides at the silicon level—physically isolated from the Normal World and inaccessible to the untrusted appli-cation code—it cannot be extracted, traced, or tampered with through software-based attacks originating outside the Secure World. This hardware-enforced isolation removes the need for additional discrete security hardware, directly reducing the Bill of Materials (BoM) while preserving a level of key protection comparable to that of a dedicated SE/HSM. The key never leaves the Secure World during nor-mal operation: all operations that require the key—hashing, HMAC computation, and verification—are performed inter-nally by the Secure World, and only the resulting output (e.g., a hashed value) is passed to the Normal World or transmitted externally. 

4.4.3. Key Usage during Remote Attestation 

The symmetric key is central to the remote attestation protocol shown in Figure 4. When the Distribution Management System sends an attestation request with a fresh random nonce, the meter’s Secure World hashes the relevant sensitive data using a Merkle tree and generates only the root for transmission, which also keeps communication overhead low—a helpful property for a bandwidth- and energy-constrained device. This root is then authenticated using HMAC with the device’s symmetric key, producing a keyed hash that binds the attestation response to both the fresh nonce and the 

A. Shakiba et al.: Preprint submitted to Elsevier Page 12 of 21 Remote Attestation of Smart Meters 

specific device key. The resulting HMAC value, together with the hashed data, is transmitted to the DMS, which independently verifies the value using its own copy of the corresponding key material. A successful match confirms that the data originated from a genuine, uncompromised device; any mismatch causes the attestation to fail. Put simply, HMAC provides integrity and authenticity for the attestation response. Confidentiality is not required for the attestation metadata in the present design; however, if a deployment requires confidential attestation reports, an authenticated-encryption mechanism can be incorporated. There is no need for the heavier asymmetric key exchange, and the hardware isolation offered by the TEE is leveraged. 

4.4.4. Rationale for Symmetric Attestation Keys 

The selection of symmetric attestation keys follows from the operating regime of the target platform, in which a fresh tag is produced on every reporting interval and per-attestation cost is therefore incurred continuously. A keyed hash requires a small constant number of compression-function calls and a single block of working state, which keeps the secure-world footprint at 2760 bytes of static and 22564 bytes of dynamic RAM even for a 512-leaf tree (Sec-tion 5.4), leaving headroom within the memory budget of the metering hardware. Certificate-based and blockchain-based attestation offer complementary properties, notably public verifiability and independence from a shared secret held by the verifier. These solutions are well suited to settings where endpoints can accommodate asymmetric arithmetic and, in the case of a blockchain, the associated communication latency and transaction overhead. The present design targets the opposite end of that spectrum, favouring a minimal on-device trusted computing base and a smaller overhead per attestation. The corresponding trade-off is that complexity moves from computation to key management, which is ad-dressed in Section 5.7.5. In addition, each key is unique to a single device and never leaves the secure world, hence, the effect of a compromise remains confined to that device.

5.1. Security Comparison 

We conduct a comprehensive comparison of the pro-posed method with similar approaches, examining differ-ent parameters and perspectives. In particular, we focus on Merkel tree leaves and investigate execution times for different types of smart meters. The Unified CS-based Framework [18] integrates com-pression and authentication within Advanced Metering In-frastructure (AMI). Utilizing compressive sensing (CS) with residual error-based hypothesis testing, this frame-work achieves energy efficiency, reduced computational complexity, and low authentication error rates. However, it faces notable challenges, such as potential fidelity loss due to lossy compression, limited adaptability to diverse attack scenarios, and deployment complexity in real-world environments. In contrast, our proposed remote attestation method leverages a Merkle Tree structure within a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) using OP-TEE, ensuring robust data integrity and privacy without compromising data fidelity. Unlike the Unified CS-based Framework, our approach avoids lossy compression, thereby maintaining the accuracy of energy consumption data. Additionally, the implementation within a secure TEE simplifies deployment complexities and enhances adaptability to various attack vectors, making our solution more resilient and easier to integrate into existing smart grid systems. Logical Key Hierarchy (LKH) [1] is recognized for its scalable key management utilizing a tree-based structure in dynamic smart grid environments. However, the frame-work encounters significant drawbacks, including resource-intensive rekeying processes during node compromises and increased complexity in multicast group management. Our method addresses these limitations by eliminating the need for complex key hierarchies through Merkle Trees for data integrity and authentication. The Merkle Tree approach in-herently provides a scalable verification mechanism without the overhead associated with key management in LKH. Furthermore, executing all critical operations within a TEE ensures that key management and data authentication are securely handled, reducing the risk of resource exhaustion and simplifying the overall security architecture. Physically Unclonable Functions (PUFs) [2] offer hardware-oriented authentication by generating unique cryptographic keys from inherent hardware randomness, providing re-sistance against impersonation attacks and reducing data transmission overhead. Despite these strengths, PUF-based systems are often hindered by high costs, real-time compu-tational demands, and integration challenges within existing AMI infrastructures. Our proposed authentication method mitigates these issues by utilizing a Merkle Tree within a secure TEE, which is both cost-effective and computation-ally efficient. Our approach can be implemented on standard smart meters with minimal modifications. Additionally, the secure execution environment of OP-TEE ensures that authentication processes are both swift and reliable, making our solution more practical for widespread deployment in resource-constrained smart grid systems.

5.2. OP-TEE Environment 

The Open Portable Trusted Execution Environment (OP-TEE) is an open-source TEE widely used to develop TEE-based solutions. While OP-TEE is primarily based on Arm Trust Zone technology, it implements Global Platform’s TEE Internal Core API v1.3.1 specification, making it com-patible with other TEE solutions. We use OP-TEE in a virtual Arm environment for our implementation portability, which allows OP-TEE to function across different architectures and hardware configurations, supporting multiple operating sys-tems and trusted execution environments. As an open-source platform, OP-TEE offers transparency, customization, and ongoing development by a broad community of develop-ers. Its compatibility with ARM processors equipped with TrustZone technology allows for broad deployment across various devices, while its adherence to the GlobalPlatform APIs ensures interoperability with secure applications. In addition, OP-TEE emphasizes security through features such as secure boot, isolated execution, and efficient memory management, making it an ideal solution for embedded sys-tems that require a compact, reliable, and versatile execution environment.

5.5. Failure Resistance Analysis 

In this section, we evaluate the system’s resilience against various failure modes affecting different smart grid components. 

5.5.1. Power Outage Scenario 

In the event of a complete power outage across the smart grid, integrating a battery and selecting non-volatile storage within the smart meter ensure uninterrupted data storage. The critical meter information is securely saved and pre-served, preventing data loss during the outage. Upon power restoration, the system seamlessly reestablishes a remote attestation between the server and the smart meter. This process guarantees the continued integrity and reliability of the smart grid by ensuring that all previously stored data remains intact and trustworthy. 

5.5.2. Communication Disruption Scenario 

Another critical failure mode involves the disconnection of the communication network between the smart meter and the server. Thanks to the implementation of a TEE within the smart meter, measured data remains secure. The TEE operates in a secure, isolated space, protecting the

data from unauthorized access or tampering, safeguarding data integrity, and maintaining privacy. Eventually, when the network connection is restored, the meter updates the server with the measurements. These failure resistance mechanisms collectively estab-lish a comprehensive and resilient framework for managing smart meter data securely. By addressing both power and communication failures, the system ensures continuous op-eration and data integrity, even under adverse conditions. This robustness not only enhances the reliability of individ-ual smart meters but also fortifies the overall security and efficiency of the entire smart grid infrastructure. 

5.6. Adversary and Attack Scenarios 

As critical components of smart grids, smart meters measure and transmit sensitive household energy consump-tion data, making them a potential target for malicious adversaries. This section outlines the adversarial model, capturing the capabilities and intentions of attackers who aim to compromise the integrity, authenticity, and privacy of smart meter data. By leveraging a Merkle Tree-based authentication method and the secure environment of a TEE, the proposed system addresses threats such as data tamper-ing, forgery, and replay attacks. The model also considers advanced threats targeting the TEE and ensures robust coun-termeasures to maintain secure communication and reliable data verification, enhancing the overall security of the smart grid infrastructure. 

5.6.1. Man-in-the-Middle 

The proposed remote attestation method for smart meters mitigates the risk of Man-in-the-Middle (MITM) attacks by utilizing a pre-shared symmetric key between each meter and the server. The key is fed to the HMAC operations so that only the authentic meter and server can generate a correct message. It is provisioned at manufacturing time and held in the secure storage of the TEE, and it is never exported to the normal world. In an MITM attack, an adversary intercepts communication between a smart meter and the central server, potentially altering or forging messages to compromise data integrity. The method addresses this by organizing transmitted data into a Merkle Tree, where the root hash acts as a compact and immutable representation of the entire dataset. Any modification to the transmitted data during an attack results in a mismatch in the Merkle root, making tampering immediately detectable during ver-ification. Without the per-device key, an adversary cannot construct a leaf that validates against the published root, and the freshness input included in each tag prevents a previously observed message from being replayed in a later epoch. Should a device be compromised, the device-specific key is blacklisted by the DMS, so that any future messages are rejected. Additionally, all operations involved in com-puting the Merkle root and verifying hashes are performed within the TEE, ensuring the integrity of the process. These hash-based mechanisms, combined with the TEE’s secure execution, prevent adversaries from injecting or modifying data undetected, thereby safeguarding the authenticity and integrity of smart meter communications against MITM attacks. 

5.6.2. Replay Attack 

The proposed remote attestation method for smart me-ters mitigates replay attacks by putting a current timestamp 

𝑇 𝑠 in the server request and integrating a server-generated random Nonce into the HMAC computation of the Merkle root. Replay attacks involve an adversary intercepting and retransmitting a previously valid message to impersonate a legitimate disrupt system operations. In order to compensate for this, the central server generates a fresh, random Nonce for each attestation session. This Nonce is concatenated with the Merkle root and processed using HMAC with a symmetric key, ensuring a secure and unique attestation signature for every session. The process begins with the server generating an at-testation request. The timestamp in this message prevents old requests to be replayed. In addition, the meter does not replay to a request with repetitive 𝑇 𝑠 . The server also sends a freshly generated Nonce to the smart meter. The smart meter concatenates the received Nonce with the computed Merkle root and applies HMAC using a pre-shared symmetric key. The resulting HMAC serves as a cryptographic proof that securely binds the sensitive data (represented by the Merkle root) to the session-specific Nonce. The smart meter transmits the concatenated Merkle root and Nonce, along with the computed HMAC, back to the server. Upon receiving the response, the server verifies the HMAC by replicating the concatenation and HMAC com-putation using the Nonce it issued and the shared sym-metric key. The attestation is considered valid if the cal-culated HMAC matches the one received. Replay attempts fail because the server-generated Nonce is unique for every session, and a previously valid message would produce an incorrect HMAC when verified with a new Nonce. This approach ensures the security of the remote attesta-tion process by effectively binding sensitive data to a server-controlled session-specific Nonce. By employing HMAC with a symmetric key and enforcing Nonce uniqueness, the system guarantees the integrity and authenticity of smart meter communications while robustly preventing replay at-tacks. 

5.6.3. Denial of Service (DOS) 

Our proposed method is immune to DoS attacks because only authorized entities can generate valid requests. The verification of each request is a lightweight process, and the requests are rate-limited. In particular, the server includes a timestamp and a keyed HMAC in its initial request for attestation. An attacker cannot manipulate server requests because the verification process is straightforward on the smart meter side. In addition, the meter does not respond to requests with repeated timestamps. Verifying smart meter responses is also lightweight on the server side because the server drops messages with old Nonces, and HMAC calculation is efficient. By the way, our proposal is agnostic

5.6.4. Other Attacks 

Our method is resilient to other potential attacks, in-cluding eavesdropping (due to encrypted communication), physical tampering (protected by the TEE), and key manage-ment issues (ensured through secure pre-shared symmetric keys). The approach also safeguards against malware or firmware exploits (because of attestation and verification of updates), enhancing smart grid systems’ overall security and reliability. This comprehensive defense mechanism makes the system robust against various adversarial scenarios, en-suring trust and reliability in smart meter communications. 

5.7. The Smart Meter Lifecycle 

A smart meter passes several stages before it ever re-ports a reading, and each stage introduces its own trust boundary. Understanding this lifecycle clarifies where our scheme places its trust and where an adversary might try to intervene. 

5.7.1. Manufacturing and Hardware Assembly 

The metering module, the MCU, and the supporting board are assembled in this stage. For our approach to work, the hardware must support TrustZone—e.g., be equipped with an ARM Cortex-M processor—so that a hardware-isolated execution environment is available from the very beginning. 

5.7.2. Firmware and Cryptographic Key Provisioning 

Once the hardware is ready, the initial firmware is in-stalled, and each meter is assigned a unique device-specific symmetric attestation key during a trusted manufacturing or commissioning process. The device-side key is provisioned into the TrustZone-backed secure storage of the TEE rather than being stored in general-purpose flash. The correspond-ing key record is securely registered with the Distribution Management System (DMS) through the trusted provision-ing infrastructure. During normal operation, the device-side key remains within the Secure World and is not accessible to the Normal World or untrusted application code. All key-dependent attestation operations, including HMAC generation, are per-formed within the TEE. This hardware-enforced isolation mitigates key-extraction risks arising from Normal-World compromise and ordinary software-based attacks, while en-abling subsequent attestation rounds without exposing the device secret. 

5.7.3. Device Identity and Factory Enrollment 

Before leaving the factory, each meter is assigned a unique device identifier and enrolled in the utility’s asset-management system. This binding between identifier and provisioned key gives every unit a distinct, verifiable iden-tity, which is what allows the operator to tell one meter apart from another and to reason about which devices are legitimately part of the network. 

5.7.4. Trusted Installation and Commissioning 

Deployment should be carried out by trusted installers following defined procedures, not treated as a purely me-chanical task. During commissioning the meter is physically connected, activated, and onboarded to the network, and its identity is checked against the records created at enrollment. Restricting who can install and activate a device closes a gap that would otherwise let a tampered or counterfeit unit slip into the system unnoticed. 

5.7.5. Key Lifecycle Management: Rotation and Revocation 

A symmetric key is only as safe as the process that manages it over time. In our model the operator can rotate keys periodically and blacklist the key of any meter that is decommissioned or found to be compromised. When a meter leaves the network for either reason, it can no longer produce a valid proof. This keeps the integrity structure consistent with the current and trusted set of devices. 

5.7.6. Trust Assumptions and Attack Surface Across the Lifecycle 

Taken together, these stages define who is trusted and when. We assume the manufacturer and the provisioning process are trusted, and that the secret key is protected inside the TEE from the moment it is generated. The main residual exposure lies in the handling and logistics window before installation and in the correctness of the installation step itself, which is why device identity checks and trusted commissioning matter as much as the runtime protections. The remainder of the paper focuses on the operational stage, where attestation and the Merkle tree defend data integrity once the meter is live. 

## 6. Future Work 

Our main goal is to ensure the integrity of smart grids by using secure computing such as Trusted Execution En-vironment. Considering the resource constrained devices, in this paper we have shown the feasibility of remote attestation in such devices by strengthening the attestation techniques with Merkle tree. However, this constraint does not apply to all other smart grid devices, and in our future work we will present hybrid attestation techniques to ensure integrity in a variety of other smart grid devices. 

## 7. Conclusion 

In this paper, we evaluated a standard smart meter with its built-in security features and found that these were insuffi-cient. Therefore, we searched for a trustworthy execution en-vironment that could support routine tasks while enhancing security. This led us to implement a smart electricity meter in the OP-TEE secure environment that achieves a higher level of security than conventional smart electricity meters. The OP-TEE environment is based on ARM TrustZone, which provides us with a secure world and a normal world for oper-ation. Nevertheless, our goal was even higher security. In this

The server will verify this protocol to establish commu-nication and ensure that any change to the hashed data will cause the remote attestation to fail. We also used a Merkle Tree to aggregate and hash data from smart meters. We tested the Merkle Tree with different leaf counts, mapped it to a smart meter, and requested the hashed data from the server. The Merkle root sent to the server requires successful remote attestation before transmission.
